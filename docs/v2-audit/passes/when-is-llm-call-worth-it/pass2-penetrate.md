# Pass 2 — Penetrate

Building on pass1: the article presents itself as a *product-engineering* framework — "when should a SHIPPED PRODUCT make an LLM call?" Its worked example (pass1 §"Application to event-vendor") is entirely about the proposal tool's runtime: proposal descriptions, brief parsing, change-request summaries. This pass surfaces the deeper thesis, the assumptions it takes for granted, its internal tensions, and — the net-new move — the category error that decides how it maps onto *our* harness.

## 1. The real thesis is narrower than the title, and that matters
Pass1 framed the thesis as "build the boring version first, spend an LLM call only where language/ambiguity is the value." But penetrating the 7 gates (pass1 §"7-gate framework") reveals the actual organizing axis is not "LLM vs no-LLM" — it is **"is there a formally verifiable correct answer?"** Gate 1 (specifiable rules), Gate 3 (number/date/boolean vs language), Gate 6 (can you define correct), the entire "When NOT to use" list (pass1: tax, pricing, inventory, audit), and the Boring Software Principle all collapse to a single discriminator: *if correctness is decidable, determinism wins; if correctness is a judgment, an LLM is admissible.* The other gates (cost, latency, volume, FOMO) are not co-equal tests — they are **economic filters applied after** the correctness test passes. The framework is really two-layered: a hard *capability* gate (verifiable? → no LLM) followed by soft *economic* gates (affordable here? at this latency? at this volume?). The article flattens these into a linear "stop at first NO" list, which hides that Gate 1/3/6 are categorical and Gate 4/5/7 are contextual. (net-new)

## 2. Hidden assumption: the LLM is a PRODUCT FEATURE, not the DEVELOPMENT SUBSTRATE
This is the load-bearing assumption the whole article takes for granted and never states. Every gate assumes the LLM call is *inside the user's product path* — it costs the vendor money per end-user transaction (pass1 §Gate 4, §Cost Model), it sits in a synchronous UX latency budget the *end user* experiences (pass1 §Gate 5, §Latency thresholds), and its failure is *a customer-facing defect* (pass1 §Fallback obligation). That frame is correct for the event-vendor *product*. It is **the wrong frame for an agent harness**, where:
- The LLM is not a feature; it is the *worker that builds the software*. There is no "deterministic version of the developer" to ship first.
- Cost is amortized over development, not per end-user transaction — the Gate-4 unit economics (per-seat, per-transaction, 10%-of-revenue heuristic, 50–65% gross margin) do not transfer at all.
- Latency is the developer's wait, not a UX tax on a paying customer — the 100ms/500ms/2s/5s abandonment thresholds (pass1 §Latency) are calibrated to end users, not to an engineer running `/cr`.
The article never distinguishes these two uses of "LLM call." For a V2 *harness* audit, this is the single most important thing to extract: **the framework's correctness/testability/structured-output/fallback/reversibility doctrine transfers; its cost/latency/volume/margin doctrine largely does not.** (net-new — this distinction is the lever for pass3)

## 3. Internal tension: "stop at first NO" vs. the gray areas vs. routing
Pass1 §"7-gate framework" says work in order, *stop at the first NO* — a clean decision tree. But pass1 §"Gray Areas" then says classification, extraction, recommendation, and autocomplete have **no stable answer** — "start with LLM; migrate if scale demands it." And pass1 §"80/20 routing" says the production answer is *both at once* (cheap model for 90%, frontier for 10%). These three sections are in quiet conflict: the gates pretend the decision is binary and static; the gray areas and routing reveal it is a **continuous, volume-dependent, and often hybrid** decision that changes as a feature scales. The honest reading: the 7 gates are a good *first-draft filter for a greenfield feature*, but the real production discipline is the routing/escalation architecture, which the gate structure under-weights by burying it inside Gate 4. (net-new)

## 4. What the author takes for granted (assumptions worth naming)
- **That "deterministic" and "LLM" are cleanly separable.** Pass1's own best use cases (NL-interface-over-structured-data, structured extraction) are *hybrids* where an LLM does intent/extraction and deterministic code does execution/validation. The interesting systems are not "LLM or not" — they are *where you draw the boundary between the probabilistic core and the deterministic shell.* The article gestures at this ("LLM as the interface, computation deterministic") but never makes boundary-placement the primary design act, which it is.
- **That evals are affordable and a golden set exists.** Pass1 §Testing and §"20–30% of budget" assume you can construct a golden dataset and a judge model. For low-volume, high-variability outputs (e.g. a single floral proposal's prose) there may be *no ground truth* and *no volume* to compute a meaningful pass rate. The statistical-eval machinery silently assumes scale the worked example doesn't have.
- **That the cited numbers are stable facts.** The 28% Gartner figure, 50–65% margins, $300K self-hosting crossover, 3–5x output multiplier, 2–5 s E2E — all are point-in-time and provider-dependent (pass1 tagged these fact-as-cited). The framework's *structure* is durable; its *constants* are not. An audit that inherits the constants will rot the way the article's own "GPT-4-class" anchoring already shows. (net-new)
- **That the human-in-the-loop is free.** Pass1 §Copilot and §Co-pilot treat "a human reviews and corrects" as the move that makes probabilistic error acceptable. It silently assumes the human's review attention is abundant and reliable. At agent scale (parallel sessions, `/queue` batches) the *reviewer* becomes the bottleneck and the least-tested component — the article's safety net is the thing it never examines.

## 5. The strongest, most transferable idea is almost an aside
Pass1 §"How AI-native companies decide" buries the single most harness-relevant principle in its last bullet: **the reversibility gate for autonomous actions** — write to a staging buffer, require human confirmation for irreversible ops. The article treats this as one production pattern among many. But for an *agentic* system (which a harness is, and the product mostly isn't), reversibility is not one gate among seven — it is *the* gate, because an agent *takes actions* rather than *emits text for a human to read*. The article's product framing causes it to under-rank the one principle that matters most for the agent case. This inversion — product-framing demotes the agent-critical idea — is the central finding of this pass and the hinge for pass3. (net-new)

## 6. Contradiction the article doesn't resolve: testability vs. the high-value cases
Gate 6 (pass1) says: if you can't test it, don't put it in production. The high-value cases (pass1 §"Clear use cases": drafting personalized prose, summarizing into a narrative) are precisely the *least testable* — "variation is the feature," there is no contract beyond "valid JSON + reads well." The article resolves this only by retreating to structured-output validation ("test the shape, not the content"), which tests that the call *ran*, not that it was *good*. So Gate 6 quietly weakens from "can you verify correctness" to "can you verify well-formedness" exactly where the value is highest. The framework is most confident where LLMs add least value (verifiable tasks it tells you to avoid) and weakest where they add most (unverifiable judgment it can only shape-check). (net-new)

## Deeper thesis (pass-2 synthesis)
The article is not really a framework for "when to call an LLM." It is a framework for **drawing the boundary between a probabilistic core and a deterministic shell, and for paying the price of that boundary (validation, fallback, evals, reversibility) honestly.** Its decision tree is a teaching scaffold; its durable content is the *shell doctrine*: structured-output-as-typed-function-call, mandatory fallback, compounding-reliability math, evals-in-CI, and the reversibility gate. Read for a *product*, the cost/latency/margin gates dominate. Read for a *harness*, those gates mostly fall away and the shell doctrine — plus the under-ranked reversibility gate — is what survives. Pass3 applies exactly that split to our ground-truth map.
