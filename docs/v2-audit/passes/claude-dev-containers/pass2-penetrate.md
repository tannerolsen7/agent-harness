# Pass 2 — Penetrate

Building on pass1: pass-1 §1 recorded the article's surface thesis ("the egress firewall is the real safety mechanism, the container is not the boundary") and pass-1 §8 recorded its load-bearing verdict ("Candidate, not Adopted — follow-on hardening, not blocking"). This pass goes under those. It asks: what is the article *actually arguing* beneath the four-mechanism inventory, what does it assume without saying, and where does it contradict itself or the ground-truth.

---

## 1. The deeper thesis: the article is a "decline-to-build" memo wearing a "how-to-build" costume

Building on pass-1 §3–§4 (which read as an enthusiastic mechanism inventory) and pass-1 §8 (the "not yet" verdict): the article's true structure is a *negative recommendation written in the affirmative voice.* Eleven Claims-Ledger rows are rated **Verified**; the four synthesis sections read like an adoption guide; the Design Challenge dares you to actually write the firewall. And yet every applied verdict lands on **don't, not now.** The mechanism enthusiasm is doing rhetorical work: it earns the right to say "no" by proving the author understood exactly what they're declining.

The hidden thesis is therefore not "dev containers are good." It is: **"The single most valuable thing in this entire feature — image-baked, agent-unreachable policy — is the one thing you can extract WITHOUT adopting the feature."** The article never states this directly, but it is the only conclusion consistent with (a) rating `managed-settings.json` as "load-bearing" and a genuine trust-boundary inversion, while (b) recommending against the container that houses it. Pass-3 must surface this as the article's real payload, because it is the one finding that survives the "Candidate, not Adopted" verdict.

## 2. The hidden assumption that quietly props up the whole verdict: "the threat hasn't materialized, so the control isn't warranted"

Building on pass-1 §8's "the threat model has not materialized in the 4-task and early 10-task batch runs": this is the article's load-bearing assumption and it is **the weakest link.** It reasons from *absence of observed incident* to *absence of warranted control.* That is exactly the inductive trap the ground-truth's own §9 Golden Rule was written to break: "If you can't name a failure mode that the constraint prevents, the constraint is overhead" — the firewall's failure mode (overnight prompt-injection → credential exfil) is *nameable and the article names it vividly*, which by the canon's own rule makes it NOT overhead. The article invokes "hasn't happened yet" where the canon would ask "is the failure mode nameable?" The answer is yes. So the article's central verdict is in tension with the ground-truth's own deletion doctrine — applied in reverse. (This is a genuine net-new observation: the article and the canon are using opposite tests, and the article's test is the weaker one for a *safety* constraint. Pass-1 §9 of the ground-truth explicitly forbids treating a safety constraint like a quality constraint — "the PocketOS incident.")

## 3. The contradiction the article half-sees but doesn't resolve: the egress firewall is praised and then defined as unbuildable in this environment

Building on pass-1 §4 (firewall = "the real mechanism," top priority if adopted) vs pass-1 §5 (the firewall allowlist "would need the local stack's `127.0.0.1` dynamic ports … non-trivial and undermines the point of the firewall"): the article ranks the egress firewall as the #1 blast-radius control AND establishes that the one workflow event-vendor most needs to protect (integration tests) is the one the firewall can't cleanly accommodate. It resolves this by waving the local stack out of scope ("accept `npm run test:local` is out of scope inside the container"). But that "resolution" silently discards a *required pre-push gate.* The ground-truth Testing section confirms `npm run test:local` runs against the local stack and refuses to run unless the URL is `127.0.0.1:*`. So the article's clean firewall only exists in a world where event-vendor's local-stack test path is amputated. The article treats this as a footnote; it is actually a load-bearing concession that hollows out the #1 mechanism. Pass-3 must record this as one of the article's real weaknesses.

## 4. The trust-boundary inversion is correct in spirit but overstated in degree — and the ground-truth already shows why

Building on pass-1 §4.3 ("a constraint the agent cannot circumvent" vs "a rule the agent is asked to follow"): the article frames event-vendor's `.claude/settings.json` as agent-editable "in principle," and `managed-settings.json` as the genuine fix. Two things the ground-truth lets us sharpen:

- **The article underweights the disk's existing defense.** Ground-truth §3e records `block-dangerous-git.sh` and a deny entry blocking edits to `/.claude/hooks/**`. The article *acknowledges* this but then says the deny block "is not protected from an agent that first modifies `settings.json` and then modifies hooks." Note: the harness already treats guard files as a hard human-handoff boundary at the policy layer — memory entry "No agent edits to guard files" (never edit `settings.json`/`settings.local.json`/`.claude/hooks/**`; surface as NEEDS HUMAN). So the *practiced* control is stronger than the *filesystem-permission* control the article models. The article evaluates only the mechanical layer and misses that the real enforcement today is behavioral + hook-based, not "an unprotected file."
- **But the article's core point survives:** behavioral + hook enforcement is still advisory/guarded-by-convention relative to an image-baked file. Ground-truth §3e's "Net enforcement picture" agrees: "Both agree the system is overwhelmingly advisory — neither has a deterministic backstop for the bulk of skill bodies, CLAUDE.md rules, or the autoMode lists." So the article and the canon independently arrive at the *same* gap. That convergence is the strongest thing in the article. It is real, and it does NOT require the container.

## 5. What the article never asks: can you get image-baked policy WITHOUT the container? (the unstated cheaper path)

Building on §1 and §4 above: the article binds `managed-settings.json` to the dev container ("baked into the container image"). But `managed-settings.json` at `/etc/claude-code/` is a Claude Code settings-precedence feature — it is a host-OS path, not intrinsically a container artifact. The article never asks whether a managed-settings file could be placed on the host macOS machine (root-owned `/etc/claude-code/managed-settings.json`, un-writable by the non-root agent user) to get the trust-boundary inversion *without any container, any DinD, any firewall.* This is the highest-leverage unasked question in the piece. It is net-new analysis: the article spent its whole frame treating policy-out-of-reach as a *container benefit*, when it is plausibly a *filesystem-permission benefit* the host already supports. Pass-3 must flag this as both an article weakness (unexplored) and a candidate gap (cheap, container-free hardening). (Confidence: directional — the exact host-precedence behavior of `managed-settings.json` outside a container is not established in either the article or the ground-truth and would warrant a one-shot doc check before building.)

## 6. The article's strongest concrete finding is buried in a subordinate clause: the shared-credential symlink

Building on pass-1 §6 and §9 (transfer table row: "`.env.local` is shared across all worktrees via symlink to same root file … all worktrees share the same *production* Supabase credentials — Does not transfer"): the article treats this as a *consequence* of the isolation gap. It is bigger than that. The ground-truth confirms this independently and severely:

- Memory: "Worktree .env.local symlink required" + "vitest reads .env.local which is prod" + "No .env credential reuse."
- Ground-truth §6 disk-only registry lists the prod-key firewall (`gen-local-env.sh`, `test-local.sh`, `worktree-create.sh`) as a genuine disk *advance* — Tier-0 credential isolation.

So there is a live, already-built Tier-0 credential-isolation system on disk AND the article has identified that the `.env.local` symlink *re-shares the production credential into every worktree* — which partially defeats the very Tier-0 firewall the disk built. That is a real, citable, net-new tension the article surfaces but does not connect to the existing Tier-0 work (because the article didn't have the ground-truth). Pass-3 must make this connection: the article's symlink finding is the cheapest, most defensible gap in the whole piece, and it does NOT need a container to fix.

## 7. Two structural blind spots the article shares with its source

Building on pass-1 §3's honesty note (reference container files and the `sandbox-environments` page were not fetched):

- **The whole piece is built on ONE unfetched comparison.** The article's load-bearing claim that the egress firewall is "the only control that operates independently of the agent's reasoning" is never tested against the *built-in Bash sandbox* (Anthropic's own alternative, referenced in "Next steps," not fetched — pass-1 §10.2). If the built-in sandbox provides egress control without `NET_ADMIN`/`NET_RAW`/DinD, the article's entire "you must adopt the container to get the firewall" framing collapses into "you should evaluate the built-in sandbox first." The article concedes this as an open question but does not let it discipline its recommendation. This is the single biggest analytical gap.
- **The article reasons about the firewall as binary (have it / don't).** It never considers the *partial* control that the ground-truth's existing `block-dangerous-bash.sh` (canon §08, ABSENT on disk per §3e) would provide against curl-exfil at the *tool-call* layer — a deterministic, container-free, already-canon-specified guard that blocks the exact "post the token to an external URL" payload the article fears. Pass-3 must connect: the article's threat-model-2 (prompt-injection exfil) has a partial, cheaper, already-canon answer (the 3rd bash guard) that the article never mentions because it was scoped to containers.

## 8. Net: what escalates from pass-1 to pass-2

Pass-1 read the article as a faithful mechanism inventory with a "not yet" verdict. Pass-2 reframes it as: **a decline-to-build memo whose three most valuable findings are all container-independent** — (a) the trust-boundary inversion is real but achievable via host-level managed-settings + the absent 3rd bash guard (§5, §7); (b) the shared-prod-credential symlink is the cheapest real gap and partially defeats the disk's existing Tier-0 firewall (§6); (c) the whole recommendation rests on a never-tested comparison to the built-in sandbox and an inductive "hasn't happened yet" that the ground-truth's own Golden Rule rejects for safety constraints (§2, §7). The container itself — the nominal subject — is the *least* actionable thing in the article.
